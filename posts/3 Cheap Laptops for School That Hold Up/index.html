A laptop for school is the hardest purchase in the budget category, because the gap between "good enough" and "wastes your money" is only about eighty dollars. Below that line you are choosing between a Chromebook and a very slow Windows machine. Above it, you are paying for weight reduction you might not notice.

Three hundred dollars is the awkward middle. It is too much to spend carelessly and too little to buy the thin-and-light Windows laptops that dominated this category a few years ago. Price increases have pushed most of those above five hundred dollars, so what is left at three hundred is either a Chromebook or a Windows machine built around a low-power chip.

Both are legitimate answers to a school workload. This guide picks three machines that do the job well, explains what actually matters at this price, and is explicit about what you give up. All three are sold in the US and the UK, and every price here is a range observed at US retailers in September 2026.

What $300 Actually Buys You

Before the picks, it helps to be honest about the ceiling. At three hundred dollars you should expect most of the following:

  • A 14 to 17 inch 1920 x 1080 display, almost always matte IPS.
  • 8 GB of memory. This is the floor, not a feature — 4 GB will feel slow within a year of normal use.
  • 128 GB or 256 GB of storage. On a Chromebook this matters much less than it does on Windows.
  • A low-power processor: an Intel Core 3 N-series chip, or an entry Ryzen 3.
  • No discrete graphics. Light 3D work and photo editing are fine; modern games are not.

What you will not get at this budget: a high-refresh or OLED panel, more than two USB-C ports, a numpad, or a machine that stays silent under load. The entry chips run hot enough that the fans are audible during sustained work.

You also will not find the thin-and-light Windows laptops in this price range anymore. That category moved up, and the honest options at three hundred dollars are a Chromebook or a thicker plastic Windows laptop.

How to Choose: 4 Things That Matter

Spec sheets at this price are noisy and several numbers are marketing. These four criteria separate a laptop that lasts four years from one that struggles in year two.

1. Chrome OS or Windows

This is the single biggest decision, and it is not a matter of quality. Both operating systems run smoothly on these chips. Choose based on what your school requires.

  • Choose Chrome OS if coursework lives in Google Docs, Office 365, or browser-based tools. Chromebooks are cheaper, lighter, and have longer battery life at the same price.
  • Choose Windows if you need desktop software that has no browser version, engineering or CAD tools, or a specific course application.

Check your school's software list before you buy anything. A wrong choice here is the one mistake that cannot be fixed with an upgrade later.

2. 8 GB of memory is the floor

Do not buy a 4 GB machine to save twenty dollars, and do not assume extra storage compensates. Extra storage is cheap to add later; you cannot add memory.

Some vendors advertise "extended" or "virtual" memory, which reserves a slice of storage as headroom. Treat it as a bonus, never as a substitute for real memory. A 4 GB machine with 8 GB of "extended" memory is still a 4 GB machine.

At this price, 8 GB is the correct target and 16 GB is a luxury you can skip.

3. Screen size against portability

A 17 inch screen is genuinely more comfortable for long writing sessions and split-screen work. It is also noticeably heavier, and it is the size students most often regret carrying every day.

For most schoolwork, 14 to 15.6 inches is the sweet spot: portable enough for a backpack, large enough for two documents side by side.

Also check the panel is matte. A glossy screen on a cheap laptop is a daily annoyance in a room with overhead lighting.

4. Battery life matters more than the chip

School days are long, and the charger is not always in your bag. At this price, battery life varies more between models than it does above it, because the manufacturers optimize the same low-power chips differently.

Look for a measured runtime from an independent test rather than a manufacturer claim. The figures on a retailer's spec sheet are frequently optimistic.

The 3 Picks

1. Acer Chromebook Plus 514 — Best Overall

This is the machine we would put in front of a student who has not stated a particular requirement.

  • Processor: varies by configuration — Intel Core 3 N-series or AMD Ryzen 3 7320C
  • Memory: 8 GB
  • Storage: 128 GB or 512 GB, depending on configuration
  • Display: 14 inch 1920 x 1200 IPS, 16:10, matte
  • Connectivity: Wi-Fi 6E, USB-C, card reader
  • Price: roughly $300 to $380 (checked September 2026)

One warning before you buy: this exact model name covers several machines with different processors and storage sizes, and the price moves with each of them. Check the full configuration on the listing, not just the product title.

The 16:10 1920 x 1200 panel is unusually good for the money — taller than the 16:9 screens most cheap laptops still use, which means noticeably more visible document area for the same diagonal size. A matte finish keeps it readable under classroom lighting.

Battery life is its strongest argument. Independent testing put it at more than eleven hours of continuous use, which covers a full school day with headroom. The low-power chip in this class also turns out quieter than most, because it does not need to boost hard to handle coursework.

Buy it if your school uses browser-based tools and you want the longest battery life for the money. Skip it if you need Windows-only software.

2. Lenovo IdeaPad Slim 3i — Cheapest Real Windows

The pick for anyone who needs Windows and would rather not pay more than they have to.

  • Processor: Intel Core i3-N305 (also sold with an Intel N100)
  • Memory: 8 GB
  • Storage: 128 GB
  • Display: 15.6 inch 1920 x 1080
  • Price: roughly $300 to $350 (checked September 2026)

These are frequently discounted well below their list price, which is why this machine shows up as such a good deal so consistently. At the lower end of that range you are getting a full Windows 11 laptop with 8 GB of memory for about the price of a mid-range Chromebook.

The i3-N305 is an eight-core chip that is slower per core than a mainstream Core i5, but for documents, browser work, and video calls it is entirely adequate. It is not comfortable for compiling large code projects or editing long video timelines.

Expect a plastic chassis, a glossy or semi-gloss panel, and audible fans under sustained load. None of that matters much for a machine that mostly sits on a desk.

Buy it if you need Windows and this is your entire budget. Skip it if you mainly use browser tools — the Chromebook gives you a better screen and better battery for the same money.

3. HP OmniBook 3 — Most Storage and the Biggest Screen

The pick when storage and screen size matter more than weight.

  • Processor: AMD Ryzen 3
  • Memory: 8 GB
  • Storage: 512 GB SSD
  • Display: 17.3 inch 1920 x 1080
  • Price: roughly $450 to $500 (checked September 2026)

512 GB at this price is the real headline. Every other machine in this category ships with 128 GB, and for a student who installs a lot of software offline or keeps large project files locally, the extra space removes a recurring problem.

The 17.3 inch screen is comfortable for long sessions and makes split-screen work practical. The tradeoff is weight: this is a laptop you carry less willingly, and it is noticeably bigger in a backpack.

This costs more than the other two, so it only makes sense if you actually need the storage or the screen. If neither is a problem, the money is better spent on the cheaper options.

Buy it if you keep large files locally or want a big screen for coursework. Skip it if you carry your laptop every day and storage is not an issue.

What to Skip at This Budget

Save your money rather than buying any of these:

  • Any 4 GB machine. The savings are small and the performance cost is not.
  • Windows laptops below 128 GB storage, unless they are a Chromebook — you will run out of room and cannot fix it easily.
  • Glossy screens on a machine you plan to use in a lit classroom.
  • Anything advertised mainly by discount percentage. A large "was" price on a slow machine is still a slow machine.
  • Refurbished machines unless the seller states the battery health and offers a warranty. Batteries are the first part to fail.

If you need a machine that will handle video editing, programming, or engineering software, budget five hundred dollars or more. Below that, the answer is a better-managed compromise, not a faster machine.

Where to Buy in the US and UK

All three models are mainstream products sold in both markets, so you are not limited to importing.

  • US: Best Buy, Amazon, Walmart, and the manufacturer's own store carry all three. Compare against the manufacturer's store first, then check the marketplace listing — marketplace sellers undercut the official price, but warranty handling differs.
  • UK: Currys, John Lewis, Argos, Amazon UK, and the manufacturer's UK store carry the same Acer, Lenovo, and HP lines. UK prices are quoted in GBP and run higher than the US equivalents, so check the UK price rather than assuming a converted figure. You will also need a UK three-pin plug.
  • Students: check whether the school has a bulk purchase program or an education discount before buying. It is not unusual for an education discount to beat every sale price.

When comparing prices, check the exact configuration. These product lines ship in several RAM and storage combinations, and the price moves with each one. A 128 GB model at a good price is a better buy than a 256 GB model at a bad one.

Final Verdict

If we had to pick one laptop for a student from this entire budget, it would be the Acer Chromebook Plus 514. The 16:10 matte display is better than anything else at this price, the battery lasts a full school day, and it costs about the same as the Windows alternative while being noticeably more pleasant to carry.

The right choice depends on what you have already decided matters most. If you need Windows and this is the whole budget, the Lenovo IdeaPad Slim 3i gives you a complete Windows machine for the price of a Chromebook. If you keep large files locally or want a screen big enough for split-screen work, the HP OmniBook 3 is worth the extra money and the extra weight.

Whichever you pick, re-check the price on the day you buy, and confirm the exact configuration. In this category the same model can be a genuine bargain one week and overpriced the next.

Every useful AI app does the same thing underneath: it takes the tedious part of the work away from you. The research you would have skimmed. The draft you would have started badly. The fifteen small steps between two systems that somebody has to click through by hand.

None of that is glamorous, and that is exactly why it is where the real value is. The person using an AI tool well is not doing anything clever — they are finishing the boring part in three minutes instead of forty, and spending the rest of the time on the part that needs a human. That is where productivity actually comes from, and it is not the part anybody demos.

Three apps cover most of that ground for a working adult: one for writing and thinking, one for voice, and one for the clicking between tools that eats an hour a week. Here is what each one genuinely takes off your plate, what it costs, and what you would still have to do yourself.

If your day is full of drafting, summarizing, recording or copying data between apps, you will feel the difference inside a week. If your work is mostly physical or purely creative, these will not help much, and it is better to know that now.

1. ChatGPT Plus — The One Almost Everyone Needs

If you buy one subscription for your working life, buy this one. It is the broadest tool here, because writing and thinking is the core of most paid knowledge work.

What it actually speeds up

  • Research briefs. Turning a pile of links into a summary you can send a client.
  • First drafts. Blog posts, emails, proposals, product descriptions, outlines. Not final copy — drafts.
  • Editing passes. Tightening your own writing, fixing tone for a different audience.
  • Code and spreadsheets. If your work is technical or you handle numbers at all, this is where the time saving compounds.

What it costs

There is a usable free tier. The paid tier commonly referred to as Plus runs about $20 a month, with a higher-priced tier above it. Confirm the current price on the vendor's own page before you quote it.

How people turn it into money

Freelancers report that the gain comes from removing non-billable work — research, first drafts, client emails, invoice prep — rather than from working faster for the same money. One commonly cited figure is around $1,500 to $3,000 a month of added billable capacity, described by the people who reported it as working more hours for more money.

The mistake to avoid is the obvious one: passing the saved time straight through as a discount to the client. Keep your rate, deliver faster, and take on more work — or charge a premium for the faster turnaround.

Best for writers, marketers, analysts, developers and anyone who sells hours of thinking.Worth it if you already have a service to sell. Not worth it if you are looking for the tool to tell you what to sell.

2. ElevenLabs — Voice Is a Real Paid Service

This one earns its place on the list for a different reason: voice work is something businesses buy repeatedly, and it is one of the easier service categories to start without a portfolio or a client list.

What it actually speeds up

  • Voiceover for explainer and training videos. The kind of narration companies need constantly and find tedious to record themselves.
  • Audiobook and podcast production. Especially long-form content, where recording time is the bottleneck.
  • Localization. Converting an existing video into other languages. Global companies pay for this regularly.
  • Audio cleanup. Tidy up imperfect recordings you already have.

What it costs

There is a free tier with a monthly character allowance, which is enough to test whether you like the work. Paid tiers run from roughly $5 a month up to around $99 a month, with the higher tiers buying more minutes and commercial licensing rights.

That last point matters more than it sounds: for client work you need a tier whose license covers commercial use. The free tier is for trying, not for billing. Check the current tier names and limits on the vendor's pricing page rather than trusting a number quoted here.

How people turn it into money

The reliable version is not "make money with a voice app." It is bundling narration into a service somebody else already wants. A small company that posts training videos every week will pay someone to produce them, and that is a retainer rather than a one-off.

Localization is the version with the larger budgets, because a company that sells in three markets needs three versions of everything, and human translation plus human voiceover is slow and expensive.

Best for people with a clear voice and patience for finishing detail. Worth it if you can deliver finished audio someone can publish. Not worth it if you want passive income — producing audio is still work.

3. Zapier or Make — The Highest Ticket of the Three

Automation is where the money is, and it is not close to the other two. The first two save you time on the work. This one lets you sell the saving.

What it actually does

It connects the tools a business already uses, so the handoffs happen without anyone doing them. New form submission creates a spreadsheet row and emails the customer. Signed contract spins up a project and drafts the invoice. Every manual handoff you remove is a small, boring, permanent saving.

And that is the product. You are not selling a tool. You are charging a business to stop doing a task nobody enjoys.

What it costs

Both have free tiers that are genuinely usable for a handful of small automations. Paid plans start in the region of $20 a month on Zapier, with Make generally cheaper at the entry point.

For a client project you are usually paying for their plan, not yours, and that cost should be in your quote.

How people turn it into money

Selling automation as a service is consistently reported at the highest project values of the common AI side hustles — thousands per project rather than tens per task, because the deliverable is a process that keeps working after you hand it over.

The entry point is unglamorous: find a business with an obviously manual process, build it, show it to them. The work is comparable to the first two apps, but the conversation is different. Nobody asks for automation by name. They complain about the same task every Monday, and that is the sales call.

Best for technical people, former ops staff, and anyone comfortable mapping out how a business actually runs. Worth it if you can diagnose a broken process. Not worth it if you want to start today with no client and no technical background.

Which One Should You Start With?

If you already sell something, start with the first one — it is the cheapest and it applies to whatever you already do. You will see a difference inside a week, because it is pure time saving on work you are already doing.

If you want a service you can start offering without a portfolio, the third one has the highest value per project and the longest sales cycle. The second one sits between them: quick to test, genuinely billable, and limited by how much audio you are willing to produce.

One note on combining them, since it comes up constantly: these three overlap. ChatGPT will write the scripts, ElevenLabs will voice them, and automation can deliver them. That is a legitimate workflow — but it is also three subscriptions for what a beginner may not yet need. Pick one, get paid with it, then decide whether the next two earn their place.

What to Skip

Some honest exclusions, because these come up a lot and they waste money:

  • Anything promising passive income. If you do not have to do the work, nobody is paying for the result.
  • Monthly income claims with no method attached. A number without the mechanism is a marketing decision, not advice.
  • Ten-tool stacks on day one. Ten subscriptions taught you nothing. One, used daily, taught you everything.
  • Anything that hides its pricing. If the cost only appears after signup, walk away.
  • AI training and AI-fact-checking gig platforms. These pay per task and cap out low, and they are not what any of these three apps is for.

Final Verdict

All three of these are genuinely useful, and yes — any of them can help you earn. What separates them is what you sell with them. ChatGPT makes you faster at work you have already got. ElevenLabs opens a billable service in a category with real repeat customers. Zapier and Make let you charge for a process that keeps working after you leave.

Start with the one that matches something you are already being paid for. If you are not being paid for anything yet, that is the actual problem, and none of these three fixes it.

Whichever you pick, check the current pricing on the vendor's own page before you buy, and re-check it before you quote a client.

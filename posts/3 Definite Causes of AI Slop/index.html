There is a particular kind of bad writing that has become easy to recognise, not because anyone decided to make it that way, but because it has the same tells every time. It is competent, fluent, well-formatted, and completely worthless. It is what you get when a machine is asked to produce volume and nobody is left with the job of checking it.

Here is the uncomfortable part: most of it is not made by lazy people trying to cheat. It is made by capable people working fast, who know the output is mediocre, and ship it anyway because the deadline did not move. That is why the causes below are mostly process failures rather than model failures. The tools are not the problem. What happens after the tool answers is the problem.

Three causes account for the large majority of it, and each one has a fix that takes about a minute.

Cause 1: Shipping the First Draft

Why it keeps shipping

This is the big one, and it is not what people think. The problem is not that AI writing is bad. It is that it is fine. Well-structured, fluent, plausible, and 80% correct — which is exactly the level of quality that survives a skim and dies under an edit.

The reason it ships is that reading AI output is comfortable in a way that editing it is not. Your own draft is full of rough edges you can feel. Someone else's draft — even a machine's — is smooth, and smooth reads as finished.

Then the reader hits the giveaway. Three paragraphs in, it has repeated the opening claim in a new outfit. Halfway down, the advice has flattened into the same sentence wearing different nouns. Nothing is wrong, and by the end nothing is being said.

The fix is uncomfortable and effective: do not edit the draft. Delete it, write four sentences yourself about what you actually think, and only then ask for help with structure. AI is genuinely good at reorganising thought. It is bad at producing it, and the difference matters more than any prompt trick.

Cause 2: Prompting for Count Instead of for a Position

"Give me 10 tips for X" reliably returns ten interchangeable tips. Not because the model is lazy, but because you asked for a number and it produced a number. Ten tips is a shape, not an argument.

Look at what you actually got. The tips are all true. They are also orderable — swap any two and nothing breaks. A list where nothing depends on the order is not a point of view, it is an index, and nobody has ever read an index for the insight.

The one-word fix

The same prompt that produces this is one word away from not producing it. Ask for the strongest claim you can make and what would have to be true for it to be wrong. Now the model has to commit to something, argue it, and tell you where it is exposed. You get four or five real points instead of ten filler ones, and you can tell instantly which ones you disagree with.

Counting is fine for navigation. A page that exists to link onward can have twelve items and no argument. But if the piece is meant to be read rather than scanned, the reader needs to find a position worth holding, and that cannot be assembled from a quantity.

Cause 3: Letting Something That Cannot Check Do The Checking

This is the one that gets people hurt, so it goes last and gets the most space.

A language model predicts what should plausibly come next. It does not open a price page, read a spec sheet, or check that a study exists. When you ask it for a price, a measurement, or a citation, it will give you one that looks exactly right — same shape, same confidence, same absence of a URL that resolves to nothing.

It is not lying to you. Lying requires knowing the truth. It is generating the most likely form of an answer, and the most likely form of a price is a price.

We hit this repeatedly while building this site. One draft of an article asserted confidently that two phones had specific battery capacities and prices that could not be verified anywhere. The numbers looked better than the vague "around $200" that would have been honest. Both of those phones turned out not to be sold in the market the article was aimed at, which meant the prices were not merely unverified — they did not exist for the reader.

There is also the softer failure. Ask for a source and you will often get one that does not resolve, because a plausible citation is a very likely sequence. Several of the most confident-sounding sources we found while researching a different article turned out, on checking, to be affiliate pages written by other AI. They cited each other.

How to check it

The fix: treat every specific output from a model as a claim, not a fact. Then check the ones that would embarrass you if wrong — prices, specs, names, anything with a number in it — against something that is not the model. If a figure cannot be confirmed, it does not go in. "Around $300" is always better than a wrong $295.

What Slop Is Not

Worth saying plainly, because this gets used as an excuse for shipping lazy work:

  • Not every AI-assisted draft is slop. A piece written by a person with a position, using a tool to arrange it, is not the same object as a piece generated and abandoned. The test is whether a human held a position at any point.
  • Not "AI wrote it." Plenty of good writing is heavily assisted. The question is whether anyone could disagree with it. If not, it is filler regardless of who typed it.
  • Not always obvious to readers. Slop is only useful as a label if you can act on it. If you cannot tell, the fix is the same: make the piece commit to something.

Final Verdict

Slop is a process failure with three causes, and none of them is that the model is bad. The first draft is fine-shaped, which is the trap. The counted list is what you get when you ask for a number instead of a claim. The invented specific is the only one that can actually hurt somebody, and it is also the easiest to catch, because every invented number can be looked up.

If you change one habit, make it the third one. Everything else is taste. That one is a fact-check.

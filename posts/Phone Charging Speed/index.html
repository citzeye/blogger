A phone that charges at 80 watts and a phone that charges at 18 watts are not the same phone, and the difference is usually not the phone. It is the block of plastic plugged into the wall.

Three things stand between you and the charge speed on the spec sheet, and all three are documented by the manufacturer rather than by anyone testing it for you. The first is that the same model ships with different charging speeds in different countries.

The same phone, two different wattages, both on the manufacturer's site

OnePlus sells the 13R in the US and the UK. Its own pages do not agree on what it charges at.

OnePlus pageCharging claimClaimed 50%
oneplus.com/us/13r"the 55W SUPERVOOC charging"23 mins
oneplus.com/global/13r"the 80W SUPERVOOC charging"20 mins
oneplus.com/no/13r/specs"Charge: 80W SUPERVOOC™"—
oneplus.com/in/13r/specs"Charge: 80W SUPERVOOC™"—

Both numbers are real, and the footnote on the US page is what reconciles them:

"Up to 80W with OnePlus SUPERVOOC 80W Dual Ports GaN Power Adapter or OnePlus SUPERVOOC 100W Dual Ports Power Adapter."

So the US page is not contradicting the spec sheet — it is quoting the speed you get with the adapter in the box, and the spec sheet is quoting the speed you get with an adapter you have to buy. Both are accurate. Neither tells you which one is in your hand, which is why the answer depends on where you bought the phone.

The charger is the bottleneck, not the phone

This is the part that catches people out, because it is invisible until you plug in a charger that was not made for the phone.

CNET put the numbers together in their OnePlus 13R comparison:

"The phone can charge at 55W speeds using the included SuperVooc power adapter, although a OnePlus representative said it can support 80W and 100W speeds, however you'll have to buy those SuperVooc charger models separately. Over the more widely available USB-PD standard, it can charge at 18W speeds."

GSMArena's review of the same phone reaches the same conclusion from the other direction:

"The handset supports up to 80W of fast charging over the proprietary SuperVOOC standard, so you'd have to buy a charger separately."

Read those two together and the shape of the problem is clear. A proprietary fast-charging standard needs a matching charger. Plug the same phone into a perfectly good third-party USB-C brick and you do not get 55W — you get 18W, which is roughly where phones were in 2020 and is noticeably slower in the last ten minutes of a charge.

That is not a fault in your charger. USB Power Delivery is a standard, and 18W is a perfectly respectable USB-PD speed; the phone simply asks for more than the standard will give it without a private protocol.

And sometimes there is no charger at all

Since 2020 the brick has been disappearing from phone boxes, and the reasons given are worth reading because they are unusually direct.

Samsung's UK support page, explaining the Galaxy S21 decision:

"Samsung discovered that many Galaxy users are reusing earphones and chargers that they already have at home even after purchasing a new phone. Our past models already have standardised chargers, and these chargers are widely available at home."

That was the S series in 2021, and it extended to the Galaxy A33, A53 and A73 in 2022. Google said the Pixel 5a would be the last Pixel to include a brick. Apple started it. The Verge's summary at the time was blunt — "The bundled phone power brick is dead" — and noted that Apple, Samsung and Xiaomi together accounted for nearly half of global smartphone shipments, which is roughly why it stayed dead.

The packaging arithmetic, which is the part manufacturers rarely quantify, was worked out by Uniqbe and reported by BGR in March 2026: roughly 50% less packaging material per box, and thinner boxes mean 70% more phones per shipping pallet.

One warning from that same Samsung page, and it matters for anyone reading a review of a different regional model:

"In-box items may vary depending on the model or the country or region you live in."

Region, not just model. A phone reviewed by someone elsewhere may not have arrived with the same things in the box as yours.

Not every brand gave up

Some still ship one. OnePlus is explicit about it on its own charging page: "Your charger is always included in the box." Its 13R box contents, listed on the Indian spec page, are:

  • OnePlus 13R SUPERVOOC Power Adapter
  • Type-A to C Cable
  • Quick Start Guide
  • Protective Case
  • SIM Tray Ejector

So before you spend anything, check what your specific model and region actually shipped with. Some of our current picks include one — the OnePlus 13R is the clearest example, while the Google Pixel 10a in our other guides ships with a cable and a SIM tool and nothing else.

A phone can ask for more power than it uses

Google's Pixel 10a spec page contains an instruction that reads oddly until you understand it:

"Fast charging – up to 50% in about 30 minutes – using 45W USB-C® PPS charger or higher, sold separately."

The Pixel 10a is a 30W phone. It is telling you to use a 45W charger. That is not a contradiction — it is how the system works. The charger supplies headroom and the phone draws what it needs, so a bigger brick charges at least as fast and never slower.

The same page lists the box contents as: "Pixel 10a / 1 m USB-C® to USB-C® cable (USB 2.0) / SIM tool". No brick. So the instruction is the whole procurement process: buy a charger before you buy the phone, or discover the need on day one.

Worth noting how much this has moved. Ars Technica's launch comparison shows the Pixel 10a at 30W wired and 10W wireless, against the Pixel 9a's 23W and 7.5W — Google raised charging on a phone positioned as a budget model and still does not call it fast.

What to actually buy

The advice is shorter than you would expect, because it does not depend on the phone.

  1. Check the box first. If a brick came with it, you may not need to buy anything.
  2. Otherwise buy a 100W USB-C Power Delivery brick from anyone. Not the manufacturer's proprietary adapter — a standard 100W PD charger will drive any phone at its maximum supported speed, and the premium for the first-party version buys you a logo.
  3. Use a USB-C to USB-C cable, not USB-A. A USB-A port or cable caps you well below what the phone can take. This is the single cheapest mistake and it is invisible.
  4. Ignore wattage above 100W. Phones top out far below it. Anything beyond that is packaging.

One genuinely useful thing about modern high-wattage bricks: they no longer need to be large. Gallium-nitride replaced silicon in most of them, which is why a 30W charger is now roughly the size of the old 5W one. The photograph below is two chargers of identical 30W output, one of each type.

Wireless is a different trade

Worth being clear about, because wireless charging numbers are quoted as if they compete with wired ones.

  • Nothing Phone (1): 33W wired, 15W wireless, 5W reverse wireless — so its wireless figure is less than half its own wired figure.
  • OnePlus 12, 10 Pro and 9 Pro: 50W wireless, which is the fastest the brand has shipped. The OnePlus 8 Pro managed 30W.
  • Pixel 10a: 10W Qi — and Ars Technica notes there are "no Qi2 magnets inside", so alignment is whatever the coil in your case happens to do.

Wireless charging remains slower than the wired option on the same phone, and with high-wattage bricks now small enough to carry, the case for it is mostly that you do not have to plug anything in.

The short version

A phone's wattage is the fastest it can go, not what it will do in your house. The chain has three links: the phone's own limit, the charging protocol your charger speaks, and whether a charger exists in the box at all. It is entirely possible to own an 80W phone and charge it at 18W without doing anything wrong.

Which is also why our midrange gaming picks lean on large batteries and storage speed rather than charging wattage — a phone that lasts all day and tops up over two hours is a worse experience than one that lasts half as long and recharges in twenty minutes, and the wattage number tells you less about that than it appears to.

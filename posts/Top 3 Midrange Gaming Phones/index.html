What "Midrange Gaming" Actually Means Now

Before the picks, the ceiling. In this bracket you are realistically looking at:

  • A current-generation mid-to-upper tier Snapdragon, or last year's flagship chip. Both are fine; the difference is thermal headroom.
  • A 120 Hz or 144 Hz AMOLED panel. Above 120 does very little for most games, but it is the single most visible difference day to day.
  • A large battery, ideally 5,000 mAh or more, because sustained performance is a cooling problem and cooling drains battery.
  • 8 GB of memory at minimum, ideally 12 GB if the chipset is fast enough to use it.
  • UFS 3.1 or better storage. Storage speed is what stops the stutter when a game loads assets mid-match, and it is the most quietly important spec in the list.

What you will not get: a dedicated cooling chamber, gaming shoulder buttons, a 165 Hz screen, or a flagship camera array. If you need those, you are shopping a different category at three times the price.

1. OnePlus 13R — The Performance Pick

This is the reason midrange gaming is still worth doing. You get a last-generation flagship chipset in a phone that costs a fraction of a current flagship, and a flagship chip has materially better thermal design than a midrange one.

What you get

  • Processor: Snapdragon 8 Gen 3 — a flagship silicon, last year's generation
  • Display: 6.78-inch LTPO AMOLED, 120 Hz, 450 ppi, Gorilla Glass 7i
  • Memory: 12 GB or 16 GB
  • Storage: 256 GB or 512 GB, UFS 4.0
  • Battery: 6,000 mAh silicon-carbon, 80 W charging — around 50% in twenty minutes
  • Weight: 206 g
  • Price: $549.99 on OnePlus's own US store, $599.99 list, promos seen at $499 (checked September 2026)

Two details matter more than the headline chip. UFS 4.0 storage is fast enough that asset loading does not stall a match, and LTPO means the panel can drop to a single frame per second when you are not playing, which is where most of the battery saving comes from.

The 206 g weight is the cost. This is a large, heavy phone, and it will show after twenty minutes of play.

On price: OnePlus's own US store currently lists it at $549.99 against a $599.99 list price, and Amazon has been running it near $499. Treat $549 as today's number, $499 as the sale price, and $599.99 as the ceiling.

Buy it if frame rate in demanding titles matters more to you than screen refresh rate or weight. Skip it if you play mostly casual games, where this much power is wasted.

2. Nothing Phone (4a) Pro — The Screen Pick

If your idea of gaming phone is "smooth screen, long sessions", this is the one. The refresh rate here is the highest in the bracket, and the panel is genuinely good rather than merely adequate.

What you get

  • Display: 6.83-inch AMOLED at 144 Hz — the fastest screen here
  • Processor: Snapdragon 7 Gen 4
  • Memory and storage: 8 GB, 128 GB
  • Battery: 5,080 mAh, 50 W (100% in ~64 minutes)
  • Build: aluminium unibody, not the plastic most brands use at this price
  • Price: $499 at launch, closer to $449 on sale (checked September 2026)

144 Hz is a real upgrade over 120 for a specific reason: games that support high frame rates get more of them, and the extra headroom makes the difference between a locked frame rate and one that hunts. The aluminium frame also dissipates heat better than plastic, which is worth more for sustained performance than another 500 mAh.

It carries the Glyph Matrix from the more expensive Nothing models — 137 individually addressable LEDs on the back. That is a notification light, not a gaming feature, but it is the reason this phone does not look like every other phone in the category.

Buy it if you play anything with high frame rate support. Skip it if you need the fastest chipset available — this is a 7-series chip, not an 8-series.

3. Google Pixel 10a — The One That Is Also Good At Everything Else

This one is a cheat, and we would rather label it that way than pretend otherwise. The Pixel 10a is not the gaming pick on this list. It is here because it is the phone at this price where the compromise is not in the games.

What you get

  • Display: 6.3-inch OLED at 120 Hz
  • Processor: Google Tensor G4
  • Memory: 8 GB
  • Battery: 5,100 mAh, 45 W, wireless charging
  • Updates: 7 years
  • Price: $499 (checked September 2026)

Google's formula here is unchanged and it still works: a good screen, a very good camera, wireless charging, and seven years of updates — long enough that you will stop thinking about the phone. The Tensor G4 is the weakest chipset on this list, and in a shoot-and-chase game you will feel it.

What you are buying is the option to do everything else on the same hardware. If gaming is one activity among several, this is the more sensible spend than a third dedicated gaming phone — if a third dedicated gaming phone existed at this price in your market, which in the US there honestly does not.

Buy it if gaming is a side of the phone rather than its point. Skip it if you play demanding titles and want the frame rate, in which case the OnePlus 13R is the same money with twice the silicon.

The Chipset That Now Costs $700

The best thing in this bracket is a previous-generation flagship chip. Which brings up the awkward part of shopping in 2026.

The Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, which is the centrepiece of our first pick, launched as a flagship silicon. An equivalent current-generation flagship now sits in phones around $700, and the midrange bracket has filled with phones using that same money on other things — better cameras, more storage, nicer materials.

The practical consequence is that the $350 phone and the $700 phone are much closer in gaming performance than their price gap suggests. The extra $350 buys you a better screen, a better camera and a better body. It does not buy you a noticeably better frame rate, because the chipset available at the low end is already last year's top tier.

That is why the first pick on this list is a phone whose successor costs more than double. It is the same silicon, and the gap it pays buys you nothing measurable in a game.

What to Skip

  • Anything advertised as a gaming phone in this bracket. If the marketing says gaming and the spec sheet says a 6-series chip, it is a normal phone with a sticker.
  • Phones below 8 GB of memory. Modern games are the first thing to run out of it, and it cannot be added later.
  • Unknown storage speed. It is the least advertised spec and the one you feel most in a long session.
  • 144 Hz on a midrange chipset. The panel cannot be fed. You pay for a headline number the processor cannot deliver.

Final Verdict

If we had to pick one, it would be the OnePlus 13R. A flagship chip and UFS 4.0 storage at midrange money is the combination that holds up in a demanding game, and the fast storage stops the mid-match stalls that ruin otherwise playable frame rates.

The choice depends on what actually limits you. If battery away from a charger is the problem, the OnePlus 13R has the bigger cell. If smoothness matters most, the Nothing Phone (4a) Pro has the fastest screen here and a metal body that cools better than plastic.

Whichever you pick, check the current price on the day you buy, and read the memory and storage specification rather than assuming it matches the headline.

Every flagship phone has a brightness number, and it has gone up every year. The Pixel 10 Pro is quoted at 3,300 nits. The Galaxy S25 series is quoted at 2,600. Neither figure describes what the screen will look like when you are holding it in the sun, and the reason is not marketing spin. It is that both numbers are measured on a small patch of screen, and the patch is not the same size as the screen you are reading.

This is the same shape of problem as the wattage on the charger: the headline figure is a ceiling measured under conditions the product will rarely be in. Here the gap is bigger, because brightness depends on how much of the display is trying to be bright at once.

What the number on the box measures

Display engineers describe how bright a picture is with something called the Average Picture Level, usually shortened to APL. It is simply the share of the image that is bright, as a percentage of a fully white screen. A fully white screen is 100% APL. A white letter on a black background might be 2%. The standard that defines it, IEC 62341-6-1, treats full-screen luminance and 4% window luminance as two separate measurements, because they genuinely are two different numbers.

Here is why that matters. An OLED pixel is its own light source, which sounds like an advantage: it makes its own light, so why not just make it bright? Because the panel has a limited power and heat budget for the whole module. When only a small patch is white, the rest of the screen is dark, the back of the phone stays cool, and the driver circuitry has power to spare. So it can push those few pixels far harder. When the whole screen is white, every pixel is asking for power at once, the phone heats up, and the manufacturer pulls the number back to something it can sustain.

EIZO, which builds professional reference monitors, describes the mechanism plainly: as APL rises, the panel is treated as being under more stress and the brightness is reduced accordingly. The panel vendor LG Display puts it more directly still, when announcing a new OLED generation, saying that peak brightness matters but perceived brightness in real viewing environments is equally critical, and that improving luminance at APL 25% is the key thing they worked on.

So a "peak brightness" figure is describing a small, bright, momentary highlight. That is a real capability, and it is what makes a specular highlight in an HDR film pop against black. It is not a statement about a white webpage, a map, or a text message.

Google prints two numbers for the same phone

The cleanest evidence that this is not an accusation thrown at manufacturers is that Google publishes both figures itself, on the same spec page, next to each other. The Pixel 10 Pro spec sheet states: up to 2,200 nits in HDR mode, and up to 3,300 nits peak brightness.

Those are not two ways of rounding the same measurement. GSMArena, which has tested the handset, reports what Google means by each. The 2,200 nits figure is what you get in high-brightness mode with the whole display lit up. The 3,300 nits figure is for a 5% patch of white. Google is telling you, on its own page, that the headline number applies to one twentieth of the screen.

Samsung is more oblique but says something equivalent. Its Galaxy S25 pages quote 2,600 nits, and the footnotes on the comparison page add that High Brightness Mode and Vision Booster are activated in areas of 5,000 lux or higher. That is the manufacturer naming the condition: the boost is a reaction to bright ambient light, not a state the screen sits in.

The same phone, measured four ways apart

Manufacturers do not publish their measurement method, so labs do it themselves, and they do not agree. The Galaxy S25 is the clearest example, because four well-known measurements of one handset land in very different places:

Every row is a true measurement of a real phone. The gap between 2,600 and 438 is not six-fold worse screen quality. It is the difference between lighting up a fifth of the display and lighting up all of it, with the brightness slider left where a person would leave it.

The Pixel behaves the same way. GSMArena measured the 10 Pro on a 75% lit-up area and got 1,399 nits with the brightness slider set manually, and 2,351 nits with adaptive brightness switched on. Both are below the 3,300 in the spec sheet, and both are far closer to what a full white screen does.

This also explains why two review sites can disagree about which of two phones has the better screen without either of them being wrong. If one lab measures a 20% window and the other measures the full screen, they are not measuring the same thing, and the ranking can flip.

Why a panel cannot be bright everywhere

Two limits get in the way, and they are physical rather than commercial.

The first is power. A brighter pixel draws more current. Lighting an entire OLED screen at 2,600 nits would demand a power budget that would empty a large battery in minutes and make the phone too hot to hold. Panel makers limit the whole-screen figure to what the phone can sustain, and the peak figure is what the panel can deliver for a brief highlight before it has to back off.

The second is heat. Bandicoot Lab, which measures both figures separately for each phone it tests, reports the same relationship across models: the Galaxy S26 reached 640 nits manually and 2,791 nits at HDR peak, and the Pixel 10 reached about 1,496 nits manually against about 3,089 at HDR peak. In both cases the headline figure is roughly double to five times the number the phone puts out across its whole screen.

Neither of those limits is a trick. A phone that held 3,300 nits across the entire display would be too hot and would flatten the battery. The spec sheet is not lying. It is just answering a narrower question than the one you were asking.

What direct sunlight actually does

Outdoor use is the case people care about, and it is the case where the headline number helps least. In direct sun, the display is not fighting other pixels for power; it is fighting reflected light off the glass, which arrives at the screen from outside and washes the image out before the panel contributes anything at all.

That is visible in the photo below, taken outdoors on a bright day: the screen's own content is being overrun by reflection off the cover glass, and the interface all but disappears. Brightness helps, but a modest increase in emitted light is competing against an equally large increase in reflected light.

Two things help more than raw peak brightness does. The first is an anti-reflective layer, which reduces the reflection rather than trying to out-shine it. The second is how long the boost lasts: sustained high brightness in sun costs battery and generates heat, so phones cap it and drop back as the panel warms. A number that is briefly available when you first turn the screen on is worth less outdoors than a lower number that holds for as long as you need to read the thing.

How to read a brightness spec sheet

Four checks, in the order they matter.

Ignore the word peak

If the figure is labelled peak, it describes a highlight. Some figures are only ever quoted as peak, which tells you the maker has not committed to a whole-screen number in public at all.

Look for the full-screen or high-brightness figure

Google calls it HDR mode and publishes it as 2,200 nits next to the 3,300 peak. That second number is the one to compare between phones, because it is closer to the whole-screen behaviour you will actually see.

Check for the words local boost

If the sheet says the figure is achieved under local HDR boost conditions, that is the manufacturer telling you the bright patch is small and temporary. A local boost on a small percentage of the screen tells you very little about what a full page of white text looks like.

Ask whether the number is HDR-only

Several of these figures only apply to HDR content. Everyday use is mostly not HDR. A phone whose headline brightness applies only to video and games is not brighter for the map you are using to find a bus stop.

The figure worth comparing

None of this means brightness specs are useless, or that you should ignore the number. It means the number answers a narrower question than "will I be able to read this outside", and the fix is to ask it the narrower question properly.

Compare the full-screen figure where the maker publishes one. Ignore a peak number when you are comparing two phones that do not publish a full-screen figure. Treat any gap larger than about half between claimed peak and a measured full-screen number as the honest cost of that phone, rather than as a defect. And if you genuinely need to read a screen in direct sun for long stretches, weigh the anti-reflective coating and the boost duration as heavily as the brightness figure itself, because those are the two things that decide whether the screen stays readable once you are past the first few seconds.
